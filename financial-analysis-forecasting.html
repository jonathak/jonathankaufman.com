Financial Analysis & Forecasting

Financial analysis examines a company's performance, financial position, and cash flows. Forecasting extends that analysis into the future by expressing assumptions about operations, investment, and financing in quantitative terms.

A useful financial model connects accounting relationships with the economic activities that drive them. Its purpose is not simply to project numbers, but to make assumptions explicit and clarify the consequences of different decisions.

Methods & Concepts

Financial Statement Analysis

Income statements, balance sheets, and statements of cash flows provide different views of the same business. Analysis may focus on profitability, liquidity, working capital, leverage, and the differences between reported earnings and cash generation.

Integrated Financial Models

A three-statement model links operating results, changes in assets and liabilities, and cash flows. Transactions such as capital expenditures, borrowing, debt repayment, and equity financing affect more than one statement and must be reflected consistently.

Budgets & Operating Forecasts

Budgets translate operating plans into expected financial results. Forecasts can be updated as circumstances change, using assumptions about revenue, staffing, expenses, capital investment, and the timing of receipts and payments.

Cash Flow & Financing Requirements

Profitability does not necessarily imply adequate liquidity. Cash forecasts examine when funds will be received and spent, how long available resources may last, and whether additional financing will be required.

Scenario & Sensitivity Analysis

Forecasts depend on uncertain assumptions. Scenario analysis considers coherent sets of possible outcomes, while sensitivity analysis examines how individual inputs affect results. Both help identify which assumptions matter most.

Questions in Practice

What explains the difference between earnings and cash flow?

Working capital movements, noncash expenses, capital investment, and financing activities can cause cash balances to change differently from reported income.

When will a business need additional capital?

The answer depends on the timing of spending and receipts, minimum liquidity needs, and the operating assumptions used in the forecast—not only on an annual estimate of losses.

Which assumptions drive the forecast?

A model can distinguish between outcomes that depend mainly on revenue growth, gross margin, staffing, payment timing, or other variables. Understanding these relationships can be more useful than relying on a single base case.

How should a forecast be evaluated?

Internal consistency, transparent assumptions, comparisons with historical results, and regular updates all matter. A detailed spreadsheet is not necessarily a reliable forecast.
