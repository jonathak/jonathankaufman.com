Capital Raising & Financing

Businesses raise capital to fund operations, invest in growth, acquire assets, and meet financial obligations. The amount, timing, and form of financing can be as important as the capital itself.

Financing decisions involve tradeoffs among cost, ownership, repayment obligations, investor rights, and flexibility. A financing that meets an immediate cash requirement may also shape the company's options for years to come.

Methods & Concepts

Assessing Capital Requirements

Capital planning begins with an understanding of expected cash inflows and outflows, operating milestones, and potential setbacks. The objective is to estimate not only the amount needed under a base plan, but also the consequences of delays or unfavorable outcomes.

Equity Financing

Issuing equity can provide capital without scheduled repayment obligations. In return, investors receive ownership and may negotiate governance rights, preferences, and protections that affect future financing and distributions.

Debt Financing

Borrowing can preserve ownership but introduces repayment obligations and may restrict operating or financing decisions. Interest rates, maturity, collateral, covenants, and creditor priority all influence the economics and risks of debt.

Convertible & Hybrid Instruments

Convertible notes and other hybrid securities combine features of debt and equity. Their conversion triggers, valuation caps, discounts, interest provisions, and priority rights determine how the eventual economics are shared.

Financing Terms & Investor Economics

Headline valuation is only one element of a financing. Liquidation preferences, anti-dilution provisions, participation rights, board representation, and future funding commitments can materially affect the interests of existing and new investors.

Financing Strategy & Timing

The timing of a capital raise may depend on the milestones a company can achieve with available resources, prevailing market conditions, and the risk of running short of cash. Raising more capital earlier can reduce financing risk while increasing immediate dilution or cost.

Questions in Practice

How much capital should a company raise?

The answer depends on expected expenditures, available cash, the milestones the financing should support, and a reasonable allowance for uncertainty. The lowest immediate cash requirement is not necessarily the appropriate financing target.

Should a company raise equity or borrow?

The choice depends on the predictability of cash flows, the company's capacity to service debt, the availability of collateral, investor expectations, and the relative cost of dilution and contractual obligations.

What does a proposed valuation actually mean for existing shareholders?

Pre-money and post-money valuations, option-pool adjustments, conversion rights, and preferred-stock terms all affect ownership and economic outcomes. Comparing proposals requires examining the full terms rather than valuation alone.

How can a company finance progress toward its next milestone?

Financing may be staged around technical, commercial, or regulatory achievements. Staged funding can limit initial dilution, but it also creates exposure to future market conditions and the possibility that a milestone takes longer than expected.

How should competing financing proposals be evaluated?

A useful comparison considers proceeds, ownership, repayment requirements, control rights, downside protections, and the implications for future financings under several possible outcomes.
