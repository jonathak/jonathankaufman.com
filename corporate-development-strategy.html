Corporate Development & Strategy

Corporate development concerns the choices through which a business builds capabilities, enters markets, accesses technology, and creates value. These choices may involve internal investment, acquisitions, licensing, partnerships, joint ventures, or divestitures.

Evaluating strategic alternatives requires more than estimating their potential returns. Each path has different capital requirements, organizational demands, risks, and degrees of control. The best choice depends on the company's objectives, resources, and ability to execute.

Methods & Concepts

Evaluating Strategic Alternatives

Businesses can pursue similar objectives through different arrangements. Developing a capability internally, acquiring it, licensing it, or collaborating with another organization may lead to different costs, timelines, rights, and dependencies.

Technology Commercialization

Commercialization connects a technical capability or invention with a practical use and a viable economic model. Decisions may involve intellectual property protection, product development, market selection, regulatory requirements, and the choice between building a business and licensing the technology.

Licensing & Intellectual Property Transactions

Licensing arrangements allocate rights to use or develop intellectual property while allowing ownership to remain with another party. Upfront payments, royalties, milestones, field restrictions, exclusivity, diligence obligations, and sublicensing provisions can shape the value and flexibility of an agreement.

Strategic Partnerships & Joint Ventures

Partnerships can combine complementary assets, expertise, capital, or market access. Their effectiveness depends on clearly defined responsibilities, governance, economic sharing, decision rights, and arrangements for changing or ending the relationship.

Investment & Portfolio Decisions

Companies must decide which opportunities merit scarce financial and managerial resources. Comparing investments involves expected returns, uncertainty, strategic fit, timing, and the value of preserving options for future decisions.

Divestitures & Strategic Repositioning

Disposing of assets, discontinuing programs, or narrowing a business's focus can release resources for higher-priority opportunities. Such decisions require considering proceeds, continuing obligations, stranded costs, and the effect on the remaining business.

Questions in Practice

Should a company build, buy, or license a technology?

The alternatives differ in cost, time to implementation, intellectual property rights, technical risk, and control. A comparison should account for the capabilities and resources the company already possesses.

When is a partnership preferable to independent development?

A partner may contribute expertise, funding, distribution, or infrastructure that would be difficult to develop internally. The benefits must be weighed against shared economics, reduced control, and dependence on another organization.

How should a licensing opportunity be evaluated?

The analysis should consider the commercial potential of the technology, the scope and duration of the rights, development commitments, payment terms, and restrictions that may affect future uses or transactions.

Which projects should receive limited capital?

Expected value is one consideration, but the timing of expenditures, probability of success, strategic importance, and ability to stop or redirect investment also matter.

When should a company discontinue or divest an activity?

A decision to exit may be appropriate when an activity no longer fits the company's priorities or when the resources required exceed its prospective contribution. Past investment alone does not justify continued spending.
