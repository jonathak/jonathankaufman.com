Corporate Governance & Financial Management

Financial management connects a company's operating activities with decisions about resources, commitments, and risk. It involves producing reliable financial information, maintaining appropriate controls, and using that information to guide the business.

Corporate governance provides the framework within which significant financial decisions are considered and overseen. Effective arrangements distinguish the responsibilities of management and directors while giving decision-makers a clear view of performance, liquidity, and financial obligations.

Methods & Concepts

Board Oversight & Financial Decision-Making

Boards oversee major financial commitments, financing decisions, and the company's financial condition. Management develops plans, provides analysis, implements approved decisions, and reports results. Clear responsibilities and timely information support informed oversight.

Financial Reporting & Performance Review

Financial statements and management reports help explain operating results, cash flows, and changes in financial position. Comparing actual results with budgets and prior periods can identify developments that require attention or changes in plans.

Internal Controls & Financial Processes

Controls over authorization, payments, recordkeeping, and reconciliation help protect assets and improve the reliability of financial information. Their design should reflect the size, complexity, and risks of the organization.

Budgeting & Resource Planning

Budgets translate operating objectives into financial commitments. They help coordinate spending, staffing, and investment decisions while providing a basis for monitoring performance and revising expectations as circumstances change.

Capital Allocation

Capital allocation involves choosing among competing uses of financial resources, including operations, investment, acquisitions, debt reduction, and distributions. Decisions should account for expected benefits, uncertainty, liquidity needs, and the alternatives forgone.

Liquidity & Financial Risk Management

Financial management requires attention to the timing of cash receipts and obligations, not only reported profitability. Maintaining adequate liquidity and understanding financing, counterparty, and operating risks are central to business continuity.

Questions in Practice

What financial information does a board need?

The most useful reporting highlights financial position, operating performance, liquidity, significant commitments, and material changes from expectations. The appropriate level of detail depends on the decisions the board is being asked to make.

How should management respond when actual results differ from the budget?

Variance analysis can distinguish timing differences from lasting changes in revenue, costs, or operating assumptions. The purpose is to understand the causes and decide whether the plan or forecast should change.

Which financial controls are most important for a small company?

Payment authorization, accurate accounting records, bank reconciliations, and clear responsibility for financial transactions provide a foundation. Where staffing is limited, additional review can help address the absence of full separation of duties.

How should competing uses of capital be evaluated?

Expected returns matter, but so do liquidity, strategic importance, timing, and the consequences of committing resources that may be needed elsewhere. A decision should be evaluated in the context of the company's overall financial capacity.

When should a financial plan be revised?

A plan should be revisited when new information materially changes assumptions about operations, financing, investment, or cash requirements. Regular forecasting can make revisions part of normal management rather than a response only to unexpected problems.
